# Web Selling Model

Use this reference when mapping a website or page to the selling and buying process.

## Source model

Traditional personal selling is commonly described through prospecting and qualification, preparation, approach, presentation, objection handling, closing, and follow-up. Organizational buying also has its own progression: need recognition, requirement definition, supplier search and qualification, proposal acquisition, offer evaluation, ordering, and performance feedback.

Primary reference points:

- OpenStax, *Principles of Marketing*, “Steps in the Personal Selling Process”: https://openstax.org/books/principles-marketing/pages/15-3-steps-in-the-personal-selling-process
- Johnston and Marshall, *Sales Force Management*, “The Process of Selling and Buying”: https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/sales-force-management/9781000317510/xhtml/13_Chapter02.xhtml

The web adaptation below is an analytical model derived from these stages. It is not a claim that a website replaces personal selling or that every purchase follows a linear sequence.

## Map selling stages to web responsibilities

### Prospecting and traffic acquisition

Search, advertising, referrals, social content, directories, and existing relationships produce different expectations. Review the promise and targeting of the source before judging the landing page.

Questions:

- What did the visitor search, see, click, or hear?
- What category and problem awareness already exist?
- Is this traffic plausibly qualified for the offer?

### Pre-approach and preparation

Before contact, the buyer may inspect service pages, prices, work examples, reviews, process, identity, and alternatives. The site should provide the information required for a sensible first conversation.

Questions:

- What does the buyer need to know before contacting this professional?
- What information would make the eventual call more useful?
- Which unknowns create unnecessary perceived risk?

### Approach and first-screen continuity

The landing page is often the first direct interaction. Its first screen should continue the promise of the source and confirm relevance.

Questions:

- Does the page answer “am I in the right place?”
- Is the visitor's intended outcome visible before internal methodology?
- Is there a mismatch between advertisement or query and page language?

### Qualification

Qualification is reciprocal. The site helps the professional avoid unsuitable enquiries and helps the visitor decide whether to continue.

Questions:

- Who is this for and under which conditions?
- What type of project, budget, timing, access, or responsibility is required when known?
- What does the professional deliberately not offer?

### Presentation

The site should connect the visitor's situation to a defined offer, mechanism, deliverable, and useful consequence.

Questions:

- What exactly can the visitor buy or request?
- What is included, excluded, and still to be defined?
- Why should the stated work plausibly produce the stated benefit?

### Objection handling

Some objections should be answered before contact, while others require a conversation or proposal. The page should not hide ordinary commercial questions behind a generic CTA.

Questions:

- What risk does the visitor perceive?
- Which proof is relevant to that risk?
- Are price, time, support, ownership, continuity, or expected effort unnecessarily opaque?

### Closing and next commitment

On a website, closing may mean a purchase, but it may also mean sending a qualified request, booking a call, requesting an estimate, downloading a useful asset, or choosing a service.

Questions:

- Is the action proportionate to current intent?
- Does the CTA state the action and immediate consequence?
- Are lower-commitment and higher-commitment actions competing unnecessarily?

### Follow-up and service

The sales path continues after the click. Confirmation pages, email replies, booking flows, proposals, onboarding, delivery, and post-sale support affect trust and conversion.

Questions:

- Does the visitor know what happens next and when?
- Is source and page context retained in the enquiry?
- Does the next interaction continue the same promise and level of specificity?

## Non-linear buying behaviour

Visitors may enter at different stages, revisit pages, compare suppliers, consult colleagues, or return through another channel. Review whether the site provides appropriate routes rather than forcing everyone through one generic funnel.

For B2B or considered purchases, identify distinct roles when evidence supports them: user, technical evaluator, economic buyer, approver, or influencer. Do not invent a buying committee for a simple one-person purchase.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. real query, advertisement, campaign, referral, and landing-page data;
2. analytics segmented by source, page, device, and conversion event;
3. enquiry, call, proposal, and CRM outcomes;
4. customer interviews, objections, and lost-sale reasons;
5. observed page and path content;
6. general heuristics.

Static page inspection is useful for identifying message mismatch, missing information, friction, and unhandled objections. It cannot establish causal conversion impact without behavioural evidence or testing.
