# Commercial Website Selling

Treat the website as part of selling: it receives visitors with an existing intent, helps them recognise whether the offer fits, reduces uncertainty, requests a proportionate next action, and preserves context after the click.

Write for an independent professional who can combine website delivery and commercial communication. When supported by the actual offer, make direct responsibility a differentiator: one person can work on positioning, page structure, copy, design direction, development, integrations, measurement, launch, or follow-up. Do not invent a team, proprietary platform, acquisition engine, or agency structure.

## Choose the operating mode

Use the narrowest mode required:

- `review`: diagnose one page, a conversion path, or a site. Report evidence, strengths, blockers, unknowns, and priorities. Do not rewrite or modify unless authorised.
- `position`: define the target, commercial problem, offer, mechanism, differentiators, evidence, boundaries, and next commitment before writing.
- `write`: create or revise copy after establishing the commercial brief and claim limits.

When the request includes more than one mode, normally review first, position second, and write last. Preserve effective copy and existing pages when the user asks for comparison variants.

Read [references/sales-and-claims-model.md](references/sales-and-claims-model.md) when the task involves acquisition, qualified leads, analytics, conversion, ROI, advertising attribution, proprietary technology, or performance claims.

## Establish the commercial brief

Determine from supplied material or repository evidence:

```yaml
target:
traffic_source:
search_or_ad_promise:
visitor_intent:
decision_stage:
current_site_or_message:
commercial_problem:
desired_business_action:
offer:
included_work:
acquisition_channels:
measurement_capabilities:
proprietary_framework:
  status: concept | in_development | operational | unknown
  current_capabilities:
  planned_capabilities:
  evidence:
sales_follow_up:
proof:
differentiators:
price_logic:
objections:
cta:
claims_supported:
claims_unverified:
```

Mark missing information as unknown. Do not turn a plausible target, pain, or capability into an established fact.

## Position the actual offer

Decide what is genuinely being sold:

1. a website or digital tool that supports a commercial objective;
2. clearer positioning, offer, page structure, copy, or conversion path;
3. measurement and attribution work;
4. a documented combination of these services.

For local SMEs and structured professional firms, useful concerns may include qualified enquiries, quote requests, appointments, supplier coordination, ownership, recurring costs, data visibility, and time spent managing the project. Treat these as hypotheses until the target or evidence supports them.

Sell the business consequence and explain technology as the mechanism. Do not reduce the service to code or graphic design, but do not rebrand an ordinary website as a sales platform or proprietary acquisition system without substance.

A proprietary framework may be a valid differentiator when it genuinely exists. Distinguish its present state from its intended future state:

- if it is `in_development`, state that clearly and describe only capabilities already available as current;
- describe unfinished capabilities as planned, being tested, or under development;
- use `proprietary` to identify ownership and approach, not as proof of performance;
- never infer generated leads, complete attribution, ROI, speed, reliability, or superiority from the existence of the framework alone.

The framework should answer a buyer-relevant question: what it lets the client build, observe, change, own, or avoid. Do not turn the page into a technical product catalogue when the buyer is purchasing a website and a commercial outcome.

Direct access can be a differentiator. State it concretely: who writes, develops, answers, launches, measures, and remains available. Avoid caricaturing agencies or claiming that every multi-person supplier is dispersive.

## Review the selling work

Assess whether the page:

- continues the promise of its search, advertisement, referral, or direct entry;
- lets the right visitor recognise their situation and desired result;
- qualifies who the service is for and when it is not appropriate;
- makes the offer, scope, mechanism, deliverables, and next step understandable;
- supports claims with relevant evidence, demonstrations, process, responsibility, or limits;
- handles material objections such as price logic, time, ownership, support, effort, measurement, and credibility;
- uses a CTA proportionate to the visitor's decision stage;
- explains what happens after the CTA and preserves useful context.

Classify material findings as `observed`, `supported_inference`, `hypothesis`, or `unknown`. Static inspection can identify friction; it cannot prove conversion performance.

Always report what works. Do not equate more sections, more urgency, more CTAs, or stronger language with better selling.

## Write the page

State early who the service is for, which commercial situation it addresses, what the professional does, what the client receives, and what action to take next.

Build progression from the visitor's next unanswered question rather than a fixed landing-page template. A useful page may cover recognition, economic consequence, offer, mechanism, evidence, process, boundaries, objections, and CTA, but remove any section that repeats rather than advances the decision.

When public case studies are unavailable, use honest substitutes: show the real page, a deliverable, an interface, an instrumented event, a process boundary, an example output, or the professional's directly evidenced responsibility. Do not claim that reading or scrolling proves the method works.

Write directly in Italian. Prefer active verbs, ordinary business language, complete reasoning, and a natural first-person voice. Use technical language only when the buyer needs it, then connect it to an operational or commercial consequence.

Avoid generic agency language, translated SaaS formulas, empty consultant metaphors, artificial urgency, fake empathy, slogans that could fit hundreds of competitors, and unsupported adjectives such as `impeccabile`, `istantaneo`, `senza dispersioni`, or `ad alte prestazioni`. Use `proprietario` only for a real owned system, framework, method, or asset and state its maturity accurately.

## Offer and CTA

Turn capabilities into a requestable offer. Clarify, when known, what is included, what is excluded, required inputs, price logic, timing, ownership, launch, support, and measurement.

Use a CTA that names both action and immediate consequence, such as sending the current site for a preliminary assessment or requesting a proposal. Add reassurance only when it answers a real concern. Do not say `gratuita`, `senza impegno`, quote a response time, or promise a deliverable unless those terms are confirmed.

Review every navigation and contact action in the context of the page. Labels such as `ticket`, duplicated menu controls, or several equal CTAs can introduce the wrong expectation or split attention. Recommend interface changes when commercially relevant, but do not modify design unless authorised.

## Final audit

Before delivery, check:

1. Is the target explicit or deliberately broad?
2. Does the page sell an actual offer rather than competence in general?
3. Is each important outcome connected to a credible mechanism?
4. Is every factual or economic claim supported at the level stated?
5. Are measurement and attribution described with their real limits?
6. Does the copy distinguish one-person responsibility without inventing an agency conflict?
7. Does the CTA fit the visitor's readiness and explain what follows?
8. Does the Italian sound spoken, specific, and commercially useful?

The strongest acceptable copy is the strongest version supported by available evidence, not the loudest version available.
