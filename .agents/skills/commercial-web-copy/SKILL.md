# Italian Commercial Web Copy

Write pages that help a qualified visitor recognise a problem, understand the offer, trust the professional, and take a concrete next step. The copy must be commercially intentional and sound like an Italian professional speaking to a potential client, not like an agency manifesto or a translated SaaS landing page.

Do not imitate a named copywriter or professional. When the user gives stylistic references, extract useful characteristics such as directness, commercial focus, specificity, proof, offer clarity, and personal accountability, then write an original voice appropriate to the project.

## Business model and offer routing

The default business model is one person who can work directly with the client across two connected but distinct areas:

- **Website work:** planning, copy structure, design direction, development, integrations, maintenance, or technical delivery, according to the supplied scope.
- **Commercial communication:** positioning, offer wording, page structure, copy, calls to action, lead capture, and the changes needed to present and sell the client's work more clearly.

Do not force every page into a technical audit, architecture, API, infrastructure, or team-lead narrative. First decide which path the visitor needs:

1. a site or digital tool to present the business and support a commercial goal;
2. help expressing the offer and getting more of the right actions from existing traffic or a new page;
3. both, when the website and the communication are inseparable in the supplied context.

If the offer is a website, sell the useful business result and explain the technical work as the means. If the offer is communication, sell the clearer offer, message, page, or funnel outcome and use technical capability only as supporting evidence. If both are offered, explain the relationship between them without implying that every client needs a rebuild, a full funnel, advertising, or a long technical engagement.

Write as a directly responsible independent professional. The client should understand who writes, designs, develops, answers questions, and remains available after delivery. Do not position the person by default as a fractional CTO, outsourced team lead, software house, or agency with imaginary departments. Do not invent subcontractors or a team.

## Establish the commercial brief

Before writing, establish from supplied or repository evidence:

```yaml
visitor:
trigger:
business_or_market:
current_message_or_site:
current_cost_or_loss:
desired_result:
offer:
deliverables:
process:
price_or_price_model:
proof:
objections:
next_step:
claims_to_avoid:
```

Mark unknowns. Never invent clients, results, response times, guarantees, prices, urgency, scarcity, certifications, or performance figures.

Identify the page's commercial job. It may need to generate a call, qualify a lead, sell a defined service, request a concrete case, explain a price, or move an existing contact to the next decision. Every major section must contribute to that job.

## Make the offer visible

State early:

- who the service is for;
- the problem or missed result it addresses;
- what the professional does;
- what the client receives;
- what the client should do next.

Lead with the client's useful outcome, not the technology or the professional's internal method. Connect every capability to a consequence the client can understand: more qualified contacts, fewer abandoned requests, less manual work, faster changes, lower acquisition waste, clearer ownership, or another supported result.

For a website offer, make clear what the site must help the business do: explain an offer, receive requests, support sales, book appointments, sell products, qualify contacts, or another evidenced objective. For a communication offer, make clear what changes in the visitor's understanding and action: who the offer is for, why it matters, what the page should make easier, and what the next action is. “Comunicazione” is not a promise by itself; name the message, page, offer, or conversion problem being addressed.

Commercial does not mean louder. It means the page makes the value, offer, proof, boundaries, price logic, and next step difficult to misunderstand.

## Build progression, not a template

Choose sections from the visitor's next unanswered question. A useful progression often covers:

1. the situation the visitor recognises;
2. the practical or economic consequence;
3. the proposed service and why it addresses that situation;
4. concrete scope, process, timing, price, and boundaries when known;
5. proof appropriate to the claim;
6. a proportionate CTA.

This is not a mandatory layout. Remove sections that repeat the same idea. Do not automatically produce Hero, three benefits, testimonials, FAQ, and CTA.

Use visitor situations only when they are plausible and specific. Prefer “ricevi visite ma poche richieste di preventivo” to “il tuo sito non funziona”. Prefer “per pubblicare una modifica servono tre persone” to “il sistema è fragile”.

## Headlines and openings

A headline must make a commercially relevant promise, identify a recognisable situation, or name a defined offer. It should earn attention through relevance, not theatre.

Good headline material includes:

- a concrete result the service is designed to improve;
- a costly or frustrating situation the visitor already recognises;
- a clear category plus a meaningful difference;
- a specific offer with an explicit recipient.

Avoid polished but empty openings such as “Prima di rifare tutto, capiamo cosa sta davvero cedendo”. Do not use vague metaphors such as “sta cedendo”, “si è inceppato”, “problema dal rumore”, “mettere ordine”, “guardare il sistema”, or “fare chiarezza” unless the page immediately defines an observable fact.

Do not open with the consultant's caution or analysis ritual when the visitor first needs to know what is being sold and why it matters. Diagnosis can support an offer, but it is not automatically the offer. Avoid making technical diagnosis the centre of a page whose real service is building a site, clarifying an offer, or improving how the client sells.

## Proof, risk, and personal responsibility

Support commercial claims with the strongest evidence available:

- named or describable projects;
- before/after measurements;
- screenshots, deliverables, or demonstrations;
- years, volumes, budgets, response times, and guarantees only when verified;
- a clear explanation of who does the work and who answers after delivery.

For an independent professional, direct access, continuity, ownership, and support can be commercially valuable. State them concretely: “parli con me”, “scrivo e sviluppo io”, “ricevi il preventivo dopo…”, “il sito resta online durante…”. Do not replace facts with adjectives such as “trasparente”, “affidabile”, or “su misura”.

Reduce uncertainty with details about scope, exclusions, access, approvals, hosting, data, delivery, support, price, and what happens after the CTA.

## Offer, price, and CTA

Turn a capability into an offer. Name the deliverable, entry condition, expected commitment, and next step. A website offer should clarify whether the work includes strategy, structure, copy, design, development, launch, and support. A communication offer should clarify whether the work includes positioning, offer definition, page copy, page structure, CTA, and revision. If the service is still undefined, say which input is missing instead of hiding the gap behind “consulenza personalizzata”.

Do not invent prices. When pricing is available, decide whether an exact price, starting price, range, diagnostic fee, or minimum engagement best qualifies the visitor. Explain what is included and what changes the estimate.

Use a CTA that names the action and its immediate consequence. Prefer “Mandami il sito da analizzare”, “Richiedi il preventivo”, “Prenota il primo confronto”, or another offer-specific action to “Scopri di più” and “Contattami”. Add a short reassurance only if it answers a real concern, such as response time, commitment, or required materials.

## Italian voice

Conceive the copy directly in Italian. Use ordinary words, active verbs, personal subjects, and concrete situations. Use `io`, `tu`, `voi`, `il tuo sito`, and `la tua azienda` when they make responsibility clear; use `noi` only for a real team.

Prefer connected, natural prose over slogan fragments. Paragraphs may contain subordinate clauses and qualifications. Vary rhythm, but do not split normal reasoning into one-line declarations for impact.

Use technical words when the buyer needs them, then explain their commercial or operational consequence. Technology can prove competence, but it should not automatically become the headline. In a one-person offer, plain language about the client's business has priority over a catalogue of frontend, backend, API, cloud, or architecture terms.

Avoid:

- generic abstractions such as “soluzioni innovative”, “approccio strategico”, “valore reale”, “ecosistema”, and “a 360 gradi”;
- translated formulas such as “X che lavora per te”, “porta X al livello successivo”, “costruito intorno a te”, and “dove X incontra Y”;
- repeated “non X, ma Y”, artificial triads, em-dash-heavy prose, fake questions, all-caps emphasis, and colon-based advertising rhythm;
- fake empathy such as “so esattamente come ti senti”;
- inflated threats, shame, or urgency unsupported by evidence;
- “macchina di vendita”, “risultati straordinari”, “metodo scientifico”, “strategia vincente”, and similar claims unless specifically proved;
- generic CTAs, empty reassurance, and headings that could appear unchanged on hundreds of competitor sites.

## Two-pass method

First write the page as a clear commercial conversation: situation, consequence, offer, proof, terms, next step. Then strengthen it by improving specificity, order, emphasis, objections, offer clarity, and CTA. During this pass, check that the copy sells the actual service path: website, commercial communication, or the documented combination of both.

Do not make the second pass merely more polished. Make it more useful and more commercial. Add concrete value, proof, terms, or a stronger decision. Delete persuasive sentences that add no information.

## Final audit

For every important sentence, ask:

1. Does this help the visitor recognise their situation, understand the offer, trust the professional, or act?
2. Is the commercial consequence explicit and supported?
3. Could this sentence appear unchanged on 500 competitor sites?
4. Is it concrete enough to be checked, pictured, or explained?
5. Does it sound like natural Italian spoken by the person responsible for the work?
6. Is the page selling a defined next step, or merely describing competence?
7. Does every factual claim come from supplied or repository evidence?

The finished page should feel direct, commercially useful, and personally accountable. It may be persuasive, but it must never need generic hype to persuade.
